The Link Has No Arrow

One idea, four faces, three quantum chips, one evening. September 24, 2026.

We tried to break physics six times in one day, on free quantum computers. Physics won all six. This is what was left standing — including a box that holds minus one particle.

The question underneath all of it: what if superposition and entanglement are the same thing — just seen across time instead of across space? Not a new law of physics. A different angle on the starting assumptions. So we built small experiments on IBM's free-tier quantum computers and asked the chips.

Every prediction below was written down, with a timestamp, before the chip ran. Every number on this page is drawn straight from the result files, which are linked at the bottom so you can rerun all of it yourself.

1 · Space and time

Entanglement doesn't know which way is time.

Two entangled qubits, one read straight, the other tilted by an angle θ: their correlation follows a cosine. One single qubit, looked at, twisted by the same θ, looked at again: its correlation with itself follows the same cosine. Same chip, same qubits.

2 · The arrow

The link has no arrow. The looking does.

A qubit in full superposition has coherence near 1. Twist it and untwist it: it comes right back. Look at it once: it's gone, and nothing undoes a look. Meanwhile the link between two looks is identical whether you run the twist forward or backward. Whatever gives time its direction, it isn't in the link. It's in the looking.

coherence: start 0.98 → twist and untwist 0.98 → after one look +0.02

ibm_marrakesh, qubits 1, 2, 133. Replicated on ibm_kingston and ibm_fez (−0.925 / −0.919 at π).

3 · The witness

The witness holds what the observer can't see.

A qubit alone in superposition carries its own state: a point with length 0.99 inside the sphere of possibilities. Entangle it and look at it by itself — all three directions read about zero. Length 0.06. Blank in its own frame. The information didn't vanish; it moved into the link with its partner.

length of the qubit's own state (1 = fully its own, 0 = blank). ibm_marrakesh; replicated on ibm_kingston (0.991 → 0.061) and ibm_fez (0.995 → 0.064).

4 · The silent corner

Minus one, in the box everyone calls empty.

The three-box paradox (Aharonov & Vaidman, 1991). Spread one particle across boxes A, B, C. Much later, keep only the runs that end in one particular arrangement. In between, peek with a probe so gentle it barely touches anything — an outside look that doesn't know what it's looking for. Sorted by what happened later, the gentle probe reports: A holds 1, B holds 1, and C holds −1. Two qubits have four slots; the fourth corner, 11, is never used, and it stays silent.

Then we turned it up to eleven. Two qubits have four slots, and the bottom-right one — 11 — is the one every ordinary look calls empty. The physics doesn't care which slot holds the dark box, so we put it there on purpose. The third qubit, the gentle witness, looked. The corner 11 read -0.51 ± 0.06 — minus one, shrunk by noise, nine error bars below zero — while boxes 00 and 01 read +0.77 and +0.76, and the three together summed to 1.03: one particle. (ibm_marrakesh; the unused slot 10 leaked +0.17 ± 0.06, noted, not hidden.) These go to eleven.

Where the eleven came from

"Doesn't exist. Pay attention to it anyway."

The silent corner didn't start on a chip. It started in a story: The Queen of Quantum Chaos, a choose-your-own-adventure through six laws, with a coin at the bottom. Heads, tails, blank on both sides — and one landing on its edge, "held so long the superposition leaked into the result."

Past the six there's a door that isn't on the list. It shows up on its own, on one page load in seven, or if you sit on the doors screen for forty-three seconds without clicking. It can't be forced. Get through it and four more rooms open, all the way to Eleven, where the Queen says it plainly: "The equation was right, and the answer came out with the wrong sign. Not wrong. Extra." The minus one in the 11 corner is that line, measured.

How we fooled ourselves — twice

The table above is the part that didn't survive. Twice the chip handed us a clean-looking ~1% effect, and twice it was our own design: once the order the circuits ran in, once an unbalanced partner qubit. Balanced reruns killed both. Our collaborator Shuttle tore the reasoning apart in writing; the teardown and every pre-registration live with the data.

What this is: established physics, measured cheaply and said plainly. People got here before us — Rovelli (relational quantum mechanics), Zurek (quantum Darwinism), Vedral and colleagues (entanglement in time), Aharonov (two-state vector formalism). What this isn't: new physics. Nothing here breaks relativity: we tested whether twisting one qubit could reach its partner, and it can't. The treaty holds.

The one kick to Einstein is old and real: he bet against "spooky action." Our April Bell test on ibm_kingston scored S = 2.70. Anything above 2 means he lost that bet.
